# Meaning audit, round 1: `research/grayling_habitat.md` (literature claims and summaries)

Scope: "In plain words" (lines 9-33), "What the literature says about each of link's values" (lines 138-179) and
"What this is not" (lines 181-186). Capture-table and segments-log numbers were not re-checked (already done).
Rows read: every GR row 1190-1304 (quote, note, kind, stage, region, value), and the 30 GR rows outside that range
(903-925, 996-1000, 1185, 1186) for the "no source" quantifiers; `research/grayling_life_history.md` in full for the
spawning, fry, summer and winter sections. Source text checked where a claim rests on a quote: Hawkshaw and Shrimpton
2014 (pp. 14, 21, 25, Appendix F), Den Beste and McCart 1984 (pp. 34-35), Stewart et al. 1982 (pp. 31, 44), Cowie and
Blackman 2003 (p. 9), Tack 1980 (p. 18).

17 findings. Severity: **M** = changes what a reader proposing the baseline would take from the sentence; **m** =
wording, hedge or citation completeness.

## Findings

### F1 (M) line 17-18: "juveniles and adults feed through summer in mid-sized and larger streams and rivers"

The life-history file does not say this; it says the evidence is split. For it: Hawkshaw and Shrimpton (juveniles absent
below fourth order, short tributaries < 79 km unlikely, likelier in large streams; life history lines 820-833), Hagen
et al. 2026 p. 6 (larger streams used by 100-200 mm juveniles), Blackman and Hunter (yearlings in the Parsnip, not the
clear tributaries). Against it, in the same file: Hagen et al. 2026 p. 26, "Fry and juveniles remain downstream where
low gradient river margins, small tributaries, and off channel habitats provide warmer water" (line 741); Stuart and
Chislett, juveniles "almost exclusively in the pools of the Sukunka River and the tributaries" (line 723); Adsett
Creek, a second-order stream, where yearlings and older, large (250 mm) fish among them, used the creek all summer
and were densest in its steep sections (lines 792-799; rows 1266, 1267, 1284); Stewart et al. 2007 p. 18, "feeding
areas in small tributaries" for yearlings and juveniles (row 1217); Den Beste and McCart, juveniles selecting 1-8 m
channels (row 1215). Neither file uses "mid-sized".
**Fix:** "juveniles and adults feed through summer in clear streams and rivers: in the Williston studies juveniles
were in larger streams (none below fourth order) and adults in pools of the clear tributaries, while reviews and Adsett
Creek (Liard) also place them in small tributaries".

### F2 (m) lines 17-18: fry and winter halves of the life-history sentence drop half of what the file says

"their fry rear in slow, shallow water at the margins" holds for newly emerged fry (Stamford et al. 2017 p. 8); the
file's In brief and fry section add that in some BC streams (Adsett, Sukunka, Nation) fry move to deeper, faster water
and side-channel riffles as they grow (lines 34-35, 620-637). "they winter in large rivers and lakes": the file says
"larger rivers, lakes, deep pools or spring-fed water" (line 43), with Sukunka two-year-olds in deep tributary pools
and underyearlings and yearlings wintering in small tributaries the size of Adsett Creek (lines 886-896).
**Fix:** "their fry rear first in slow, shallow water at the margins, and in some streams move to deeper, faster water
as they grow; ... they winter mostly in larger rivers and lakes, some young fish in pools of small tributaries".

### F3 (M) lines 21, 147, 153-154, 184: "No source gives a gradient limit" omits the sources that name steeper gradient as where use stops

True of a numeric limit (no GR row gives one; row 1186 records the passage search that found none). But four
statements tie the end of use to steeper gradient, without a number, and none is cited in the gradient section:
- row 1266 (Stewart et al. 1982 p. 44): yearlings and older used Adsett Creek to about km 18, beyond which "fish
  habitat become marginal as a result of rapidly increasing gradient" (and lack of riparian cover after a fire);
- Cowie and Blackman 2003 p. 9, quoted in the life-history file (line 410): "Reaches with higher gradients and a high
  percentage of boulder/cobble substrate have been noted in the Table and Anzac rivers to limit the extent of spawning
  (Blackman et al. 2001)" (checked in the source text, line 595-597);
- row 1255 (Den Beste and McCart p. 21): most Atigun spawning "where the stream gradient and scour are less severe";
- row 1265 (Cowie and Blackman 2004 p. 9): Osilinka fry did not reach the mouth, "a slightly higher gradient".
And one points the other way: row 1267, Adsett yearlings and older densest in the steep Sections 3 and 4 (hedged,
"These results suggest").
Uncited gradient rows overall: 1191, 1192, 1198, 1199, 1200, 1255, 1265, 1266, 1267.
**Fix:** line 147: "No source gives a number. Three say steeper reaches end use: Adsett Creek's habitat became marginal
upstream with rapidly increasing gradient (1266), steeper boulder and cobble reaches limit spawning in the Table and
Anzac (Cowie and Blackman 2003, p. 9, citing Blackman et al. 2001; `research/grayling_life_history.md`), and in the
Atigun most spawning lay where gradient and scour are less severe (1255); in Adsett Creek, though, yearlings and older
were densest in the steep sections (1267)." Carry "no number" into lines 21 and 184 ("No source gives a gradient
value as a limit").

### F4 (M) line 22: "nearly all under 2 %" hides that the one site-level set reaches 6.96 %

The values under 2 % are whole-river or reach averages (1257-1262: 0.1, 0.1, 0.2, < 0.5, 1-2, 0.3 %) and Little Smoky
overwintering reaches (1201, 1268, 1269). The only row that places individual sites with grayling is 1263: 27 sites on
segments of 0-6.96 % (mean 1.22 %). Counting rows, "nearly all" holds; for a reader weighing 3.49 %, the one data set
measured at sites is the one that goes past it. ("Under 2 %" also includes 1261's 1-2 %, which is at 2 %.)
**Fix:** "Where gradients are given they are of rivers and reaches where grayling were found, at or under 2 %, and of
27 Williston sites with juveniles, from 0 to 6.96 % (mean 1.22 %)."

### F5 (m) line 152: 1264 cited without its own caveat

"a watershed's gradient did not predict whether juveniles were there (1264)". Row 1264's note and the source (p. 25,
text line 1922-1927) add that most study watersheds had intermediate average and low maximum gradients (< 2.6 %),
"indicating a lack of watersheds with overall high gradients", and that gradient effects "would be more evident at a
smaller scale". The test had little steep ground to see.
**Fix:** append "; most of the watersheds had maximum gradients under 2.6 %, which the authors say left little contrast
to test (1264)".

### F6 (m) line 154: 1256 loses "long" and its hedge

"the most spawning generally lay toward the tails of runs before steep sections". The source (p. 31): "These areas
generally occurred toward the tail of long runs, particularly those preceding steep gradients." The sentence makes
"before steep sections" a property of every such run.
**Fix:** "toward the tails of long runs, particularly runs above steep gradients (1256)".

### F7 (M) line 26: "spawning was seen in a 3 m constructed channel and in large rivers"

The constructed channel is seen (1203: spawners observed, 12 and 56 observations). "Seen in large rivers" holds outside
BC only: Fond du Lac River eggs found by kick sampling (1226, 1242) and Tack's Interior Alaska statement (1209). In the
Williston the large-river spawning is suspected or inferred (1207 "described only briefly", 1213 telemetry, 1270
"suspected", 1205 a critical-habitat designation); the life-history file says no spawning site in the Williston has
been seen (line 1000), and Blackman's ground surveys found fry at every suspected site except the Parsnip mainstem
(line 387). The bullet also drops the small-stream side that most spawning width rows carry: small tributaries and bog
streams (1204, 1208, 1210, 1211, 1212), bog streams that go dry in late summer (1228), Adsett Creek, a second-order
stream (1283), which bears directly on a 4 m spawning minimum.
**Fix:** "Spawning was seen in a 3 m constructed channel (NT) and found by its eggs in a large Saskatchewan river; most
sources name small tributaries or bog streams, with no width, and in the Williston large-river spawning is inferred
from tagged fish and fry."

### F8 (m) lines 159-161: the rearing width paragraph omits rows that bear on a minimum

Uncited rearing width rows: 1214 (Stewart et al.'s ~10 m class, coded `rear_channel_width_min` = 10 with `kind =
expert`, which a reader filtering the table will meet), 1217 and 1218 ("small tributaries", "small spawning streams"
for juveniles and fry), 1273 (yearlings favoured the larger Parsnip over clear tributaries), 1274 (mainstem shore
margins for Table fry). And the one statement in any held source that reads as a lower width bound: Den Beste and
McCart p. 35, adults "channels less than 6 m wide are selected against" (row 1216's note; life history line 807;
checked in the source text, line 1554-1556), a preference, not a limit.
**Fix:** add after the Alaska sentence: "In the same streams adults used channels averaging 10 m and selected against
those under 6 m (1216's note), also a preference." Cite 1214 with 1202 as the review's ~10 m class, and 1217, 1218,
1273, 1274 as statements with no width.

### F9 (m) lines 162-163: "every other source says "small tributaries" or names large rivers"

The quotation marks present a phrase most rows do not use ("smaller ... tributaries" 1210, "small streams" 1211,
"small spawning streams" 1212, "bog or tundra streams" 1208), and 1206 says neither: it says stream reaches that warm
quickly in spring, which "may also be too small" for later rearing. 1204 names both mainstem rivers and small
tributaries. 1213 names the Anzac, Table, Hominka and Missinka (tributaries of the Parsnip, 506-939 km² for two of
them), not obviously "large".
**Fix:** "every other source calls spawning streams small, names rivers, or both, with no width (1204-1213, 1270)".

### F10 (M) lines 28 and 167: "No source states one" / "No source states a MAD"

Two rows state a river's long-term mean daily flow, which is a mean annual discharge in all but name: 1279, the Parsnip,
"mean daily flow rate to 142 m3/sec from 1985 to 1995"; 1278, the Table, "an average daily discharge of 12.9 m3/sec"
(an estimate, Langston 1998). The plain-words bullet itself goes on to say flows are given "for a river's mean". 1280
and 1281 are January-August and January-September means (their notes say not MAD). What no source states is a MAD
threshold.
**Fix:** line 28: "No source gives a MAD limit." Line 167: "No source gives a MAD limit. Two give a river's long-term
mean flow, a MAD in effect, where fry were caught: the Table's estimated 12.9 m³/s and the Parsnip's 142 m³/s
(1278, 1279), beside partial-year means of the Ingenika and Nation, 75.5 and 104.4 m³/s (1280, 1281)."

### F11 (M) line 29: "from 0.2 m³/s to hundreds"

The low end is wrong: 1223 runs from 0.096 m³/s, and 1277 (Adsett Creek, where fry reared) from below detection; for
a spawning floor of 0.2 m³/s, 1228 (bog streams that go dry in late summer are used for spawning) is the closest
statement. The high end ("hundreds") rests on 1226 (Fond du Lac, ~400 m³/s at spawning), which the MAD section does
not cite.
**Fix:** "from below detection and 0.1 m³/s to about 400 m³/s, and bog streams that dry up in late summer are used for
spawning."

### F12 (m) lines 167-171: the list of flows given is not the whole set

"The flows given are of other kinds:" then five groups; uncited: 1226 (Fond du Lac, ~400 m³/s at spawning), 1227
(Red Rock Creek annual peak 1.9-8.3 m³/s), 1228 (Tack: bog streams that dry in late summer used for spawning; no
value), 1229 and 1230 (Sukunka: discharge through the pools where 2+ juveniles were, 1-15 and 21-65 m³/s), 1282
(Parsnip winter flow, 31 m³/s, overwintering).
**Fix:** add 1226 and 1228 to the spawning flows (1228 matters most for the 0.2 m³/s spawning value), 1229-1230 to the
rearing ones, or say "Among the flows given:".

### F13 (m) line 177: Stamford's fifth-order sentence loses its hedge

"as grayling limited to "cooler fifth order streams" (1233)": the source says "the Williston Reservoir watershed might
present a somewhat unique picture, as grayling appear more limited to cooler fifth order streams". Row 1233 is also
Stamford citing Ballard and Shrimpton 2009 with Hawkshaw and Shrimpton 2014, so "relay the study" fits it only in part.
**Fix:** "and, elsewhere, as grayling that "appear more limited to cooler fifth order streams" (1233, citing this
report and Ballard and Shrimpton 2009)".

### F14 (m) line 178: Adsett spawning stated without "apparently"

"grayling spawned in ... Adsett Creek": row 1283's note says "apparently spawned May 10-25"; the source (p. 31,
text line 4554) "Apparently, spawning occurred May 10-25", and turbidity prevented watching it (life history line 307).
**Fix:** "grayling apparently spawned in Adsett Creek, a second-order stream, and their fry reared there through the
summer (1283, 1284)".

### F15 (M) line 185: "the values are where grayling were seen, mostly in large Williston rivers"

Of the 31 GR rows in 1190-1304 that give a gradient, width or discharge value (kinds `dist`, `pref`, `expert`), 14
are Williston and 9 are large Williston rivers (Osilinka 1257-1258, Omineca 1259, Parsnip 1262, 1271, 1279, 1282,
Ingenika 1280, Nation 1281); with the Table (1260, 1261, 1278), 12. The rest are the Hawkshaw sites of every size
(1263, 1272), the NT (1202, 1203, 1214, 1224, 1225), Alaska (1215, 1216), Alberta (1201, 1268, 1269), Saskatchewan
(1226), Montana (1227), multi-region (1223), the Sukunka (1229, 1230) and Adsett Creek (1276, 1277).
**Fix:** "the values are where grayling were found, many of them large Williston rivers and the rest sites and reaches
across BC, the NT, Alaska and Alberta."

### F16 (m) line 186: "no spring spawning sample"

Line 122-123 of the same file counts 175 May and June visits, 11 with grayling. The point is that there is no sample of
spawning, not of spring.
**Fix:** "few spring visits (175 in May and June) and none aimed at spawning".

### F17 (m) lines 142-143: the kinds named are not all the kinds used

"a site measurement is coded `dist`, a stated preference `pref`, a statement with no value `qual`": rows 1190-1304
also use `expert` (1202, 1214, Stewart's ~10 m class) and `max_obs` (1246). And `dist` covers whole-river averages and
flows (1259, 1262, 1278-1282), not only site measurements. Counts: dist 54, qual 47, pref 11, expert 2, max_obs 1.
**Fix:** "a value where grayling were found (a site, reach or river) is coded `dist`, a stated preference `pref`, a
reviewer's class `expert`, a statement with no value `qual`".
